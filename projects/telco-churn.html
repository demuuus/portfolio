Telco Customer Churn

Overview

A supervised machine learning project that estimates the probability that a telecom customer has churned and turns that probability into a contact decision for a retention team. Built together with Reyner Elazaro Tampubolon, it compares six algorithms under one validation protocol, selects a model with a rule fixed in advance, and chooses the decision threshold from an explicit cost model.

Problem

About one customer in four in the data has churned. A customer success team that wants to send retention offers needs a way to rank customers by churn risk and to decide how many of them are worth contacting, weighing missed churners against offers sent to customers who would have stayed anyway.

Dataset

4,930 customers described by ten attributes: dependents, tenure, four add-on services, internet service type, contract type, paperless billing, and monthly charges. The target is imbalanced, with 26.7% churners. There are no missing values; the 77 duplicate rows were kept because the data has no customer identifier to prove they are the same person.

Exploratory Analysis

  • Month-to-month customers churn at 43.3%, against 9.9% on one-year and 2.7% on two-year contracts
  • Churn falls steadily with tenure, from 47.9% in the first year to 9.1% after four years
  • Fiber optic customers churn at 42.2%, compared with 18.8% on DSL
  • Customers without tech support churn at 41.4%, against 15.4% for those who have it

Methodology

All preprocessing lives inside a scikit-learn pipeline, so every transformation is fitted on training folds only. After a stratified 80/20 split, models were compared with stratified 5-fold nested cross-validation: an inner grid search tuned hyperparameters on PR-AUC, and the outer folds produced honest out-of-fold predictions. The test set was kept aside for a single final evaluation.

  • Compared Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, HistGradientBoosting, XGBoost, and LightGBM against a dummy baseline
  • Tested class weighting, random oversampling, and SMOTENC for class imbalance; none improved PR-AUC and all worsened calibration, so imbalance was handled through the threshold instead
  • Selected Logistic Regression by a pre-registered rule: prefer the most interpretable model unless a more complex one wins clearly across folds. The top four models were within 0.003 PR-AUC of each other
  • Set the threshold at 0.27 by minimising expected cost on training predictions, close to the theoretical break-even point of 0.278

Results

On the 986 held-out test customers at a threshold of 0.27, the model reached a recall of 0.81 and a ROC-AUC of 0.854, with a PR-AUC of 0.685 against a baseline of 0.267. It flagged 413 customers, 52% of whom were actual churners, about twice the base rate. Every metric is reported with a 95% bootstrap interval.

Under the project's illustrative cost assumptions, contacting only the flagged customers had the lowest expected cost of the three strategies, 11.6% below doing nothing. The precision and F1 targets were not met; the analysis showed that no threshold of any of the six models could meet both, which points to the limits of the ten features rather than the choice of algorithm.

Limitations

  • The data has no timestamps, so the model classifies churn status; it is not shown to predict churn before it happens
  • Validation is internal only, on one split of one dataset
  • The model rarely flags churners on one- or two-year contracts
  • Business figures rest on assumed retention rates and offer costs, and measure revenue at risk rather than profit

What I Learned

This project taught me that a trustworthy model depends as much on the evaluation protocol as on the algorithm. Nested cross-validation, a selection rule fixed before seeing the results, and a single untouched test set are what make the numbers believable. It also showed me that a threshold is a business decision, and that reporting limitations and unmet targets honestly is part of the result, not a weakness of it.
